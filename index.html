k-WM: Predicting k Steps at Once for Long-Horizon Planning with World Models

Anonymous authors · ICLR 2027 submission

Abstract

Latent world models have become an appealing approach to visual control, enabling goal-conditioned optimization at test time and generalization across tasks with a single model. In practice, however, planning success remains limited to short horizons, and gradient-based planners driven by autoregressive rollouts still underperform sampling-based methods, despite their potential to optimize action sequences efficiently through model gradients. We hypothesize that two properties of next-step prediction contribute to these limitations: autoregressive rollouts compound prediction errors, and planning gradients must traverse a chain of nested model calls. We study a simple alternative: predicting the entire future sequence in a single forward pass. We introduce k-WM, a minimal extension to transformer-based JEPA world models: a multi-horizon training objective and input layout that let the predictor map a variable-length action sequence directly to the corresponding sequence of future latent states in a single forward pass. We apply k-WM to three classes of JEPA world models and evaluate on four environments. Overall, k-WM improves gradient-based planning by 37% and sampling-based planning by 15% over the existing next-step world models it extends. Our analysis shows that the gains cannot be explained by parallel rollout alone: improvements persist under autoregressive inference and coincide with substantial changes in the learned representation. Parallel prediction additionally reduces long-horizon inference cost. Together, k-WM offers a simple, broadly applicable path to more accurate and efficient long-horizon control.

Method

Read the text

k-WM changes only the predictor input layout and the training objective of a JEPA world model, leaving the encoder and predictor architecture unchanged.

Method

\(k\) is the training horizon (future targets per training sequence), \(d\) the inference chunk length (states predicted before predictions are fed back), and \(H\) the planning horizon (model steps optimized).

Predictor and objective

\[ (\hat{z}_{t+1},\ldots,\hat{z}_{t+n}) = T_\theta^{k\text{-step}}\left(z_t,\, a_{t:t+n}\right), \quad n\leq k, \]
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{wm}}^{k\text{-step}} = \frac{1}{\sum_{m=1}^{k} f(m)} \sum_{n=1}^{k} f(n)\,\left\| \hat{z}_{t+n}-z_{t+n} \right\|. \]

\(T_\theta^{k\text{-step}}\) is a causal transformer, and \(k=1\) is the next-step predictor. We use \(f(n)=1\) (Appendix A) plus a model-specific anti-collapse term \(\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}}\).

Planning

\[ \hat{a}_{0:H} = \operatorname*{argmin}_{a_{0:H}} \left\| z_{\text{goal}}-\hat{z}_H \right\| \]

Rollouts use chunks of at most \(k\), and \(d=1\) is autoregressive. Planning is open-loop with CEM or GD (Appendix E.2).

World models

  • DINO-WM. Frozen DINOv2 ViT-S patch features, \(z_t\in \mathbb{R}^{16\times16\times 384}\), no anti-collapse term.
  • DINO FT. DINOv2 ViT-S fine-tuned end to end, CLS latent \(z_t\in \mathbb{R}^{1\times384}\), EMA teacher.
  • LeWM. ViT-Tiny, \(z_t\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times 192}\), SIGReg (Appendix D), actions concatenated instead of AdaLN.

Each is compared with its next-step counterpart under the same recipe. All use frameskip 5, three history frames and \(k=8\) (Appendix E.1), so one forward pass predicts 10 model steps (50 environment steps).

Short horizon

Read the text

Multi-horizon training performs on par with or better than its matched next-step baseline across most settings, despite receiving supervision over substantially longer prediction horizons.

Planning results

Goals come from expert trajectories on Push-T, Cube and Maze, and are sampled uniformly in joint space (zero initial velocity) on Reacher. Extra baselines are Temporal Straightening and Fast LeWM (Appendix D), with the same proprioception as our models.

Short horizon

Gains are largest for end-to-end encoders. DINO FT gains 21.2 (CEM) and 25.0 (GD) points on average, and LeWM gains 5.3 and 12.8. With the frozen DINO-WM encoder, CEM barely changes but GD gains 8.7 points, so adapting the encoder to the objective matters. k-WM is strongest on Push-T and Reacher. Prior methods are stronger on Maze and Cube.

Long horizon

Read the text

DINO FT maintains an overall advantage over its next-step counterpart, and multi-horizon LeWM shows a growing advantage over the original AdaLN LeWM as the horizon increases.

Long horizon

At \(H=10\) (50 environment steps), multi-horizon LeWM beats the original AdaLN LeWM under GD by 29.3 points on Push-T and 28.7 on Maze. Temporal Straightening is strongest on Push-T and Cube. k-WM is clearest ahead on Reacher and at longer horizons.

Analysis

Choosing the training horizon k

Read the text

Planning performance changes systematically as the prediction horizon increases, and different world model classes and planners exhibit different sensitivities to this choice.

Choosing the training horizon k

DINO FT is relatively insensitive to \(k\), while LeWM responds more sharply. On Reacher, success jumps at \(k=5\), suggesting a threshold in the learned representation.

Separating multi-horizon training from parallel inference

Read the text

Autoregressively trained models also beat the next-step baseline in some settings, so parallel rollout alone does not explain the gain. The effect of chunk length depends on the task and planner.

Separating multi-horizon training from parallel inference

We vary the inference chunk length \(d\) and compare with autoregressively trained models (Appendix A). The effect of \(d\) is non-monotonic. Push-T peaks at an intermediate \(d\), and Reacher depends strongly on the planner. On Push-T, the autoregressively trained models clearly beat the next-step baseline despite autoregressive inference.

What the multi-horizon objective teaches the encoder

Read the text

Ground-truth state becomes increasingly linearly decodable as the training horizon grows on both Push-T and Reacher. Latent distance also tracks distance in state more faithfully with a longer training horizon, gradually on Push-T and through a sharp transition on Reacher.

What the multi-horizon objective teaches the encoder

DINO FT has no extra latent regularizer, so it isolates the effect of the training horizon. Its linear-probe error falls steadily with \(k\). LeWM is U-shaped, matching its higher sensitivity to \(k\) in Figure 6.

We compare latent distances with ground-truth state distances while matching the other state variables. On Push-T the relation becomes more monotonic with \(k\). Reacher shows a sharp transition. The second joint is well organized at all horizons, while the first is poorly separated near 0° and 180° until \(k\) grows. Probe error drops by nearly an order of magnitude around \(k=3\), with seeds on either side, before planning success jumps around \(k=5\) (Figure 6).

Longer-horizon supervision thus makes the encoder keep state information it previously represented weakly. A linear stochastic model shows an analogous transition (Appendix C).

A · Loss weighting curriculum

The \(k\)-step loss weights the error at horizon \(n\) by \(f(n)\), and every result in the main text uses uniform weights, \(f(n)=1\). Here we test whether a non-uniform weighting profile, or a curriculum that changes \(f\) over the course of training, performs better. Since only the relative weight assigned to each horizon matters, we summarize each profile by its mean horizon. In our implementation, the loss also includes one-step predictions at the two teacher-forced history positions, which we count as \(n=1\). We therefore define

\[ \bar n(f) = \frac{2f(1)+\sum_{n=1}^{k} n f(n)}{2f(1)+\sum_{m=1}^{k} f(m)}. \]

With uniform weights, this gives \(\bar n=1\) for \(k=1\), \(\bar n=2.43\) for \(k=5\), and \(\bar n=3.8\) for \(k=8\).

Profiles. All weighted arms keep the \(k=8\) window and change only \(f\). We use four one-parameter profiles:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{cutoff:} && f_c(n) = \min\!\big(1, \max(0, c-n+1)\big), \quad c\in[1,k], \\ &\text{geometric:} && f_g(n) = g^{n-1}, \quad g\in[0,1], \\ &\text{power law:} && f_p(n) = n^{-p}, \quad p\geq 0, \\ &\text{far-only:} && f_a(n) = \mathbf{1}[n=1] + a\,\mathbf{1}[n=k], \quad a\geq 0. \end{aligned} \]

The cutoff profile with \(c=1\) is exactly the next-step objective, and with integer \(c=j\) it is exactly the uniform \(k=j\) objective. The geometric and power-law profiles decay smoothly with \(n\), while the far-only profile trains only on the first and last horizons. Each profile is indexed by \(\bar n\): at every optimization step, we solve by bisection for the parameter (\(c\), \(g\), \(p\), or \(a\)) that produces the target \(\bar n\).

Curricula. A curriculum moves the target linearly with training progress \(\tau\in[0,1]\), the fraction of optimization steps completed:

\[ \bar n(\tau) = \bar n_0+\tau(\bar n_1-\bar n_0). \]

The three ramps (cutoff, geometric, and power law) go from \(\bar n_0=1\) to \(\bar n_1=3.8\), i.e. from the next-step objective to the uniform \(k=8\) objective, so they end on the same objective as our baseline. The reverse cutoff ramp runs in the opposite direction, from \(3.8\) to \(1\), and ends on the next-step objective. The slow-tempo geometric ramp instead moves \(g\) linearly from \(0\) to \(1\); this stays near the next-step objective for longer (\(\bar n=1.48\) halfway through training, compared with \(2.4\) for the ramp that is linear in \(\bar n\)). Finally, two fixed profiles hold \(\bar n=2.43\), the value of the uniform \(k=5\) objective, for the entire run: one geometric and one far-only.

Setup. We run this study with DINO FT on Push-T, training every arm for two epochs using the recipe from the main experiments. We compare against the uniform \(k=1\) (next-step) and \(k=8\) objectives. For reference, the figure also shows two autoregressively trained arms, which keep uniform \(f\) and \(k=8\) but fill each future input position with the model's own previous prediction (one predictor pass per step, with gradients flowing through the fed-back predictions). The predictor attends either to the full window or only to the last three tokens (sliding, as in the DINO-WM rollout). Planning uses CEM (300 samples, 30 iterations, top 30) and GD (Adam, learning rate \(0.08\), 500 steps) at goal horizons \(H\in\{5,8,10\}\), with 3 seeds × 50 episodes each.

Results. No weighting profile or curriculum outperforms uniform weighting. Each of the seven weighted arms falls below the uniform \(k=8\) baseline in all six planner–horizon settings (baseline CEM \(0.90/0.50/0.37\) and GD \(0.83/0.38/0.24\) at \(H=5/8/10\)). The best-performing arm, the cutoff ramp, comes close at \(H=5\) (\(0.88\) CEM) but falls behind at longer horizons (\(0.19\) versus \(0.37\) CEM at \(H=10\)). Among the ramps, those that spend less of training emphasizing long horizons perform worse: the cutoff, geometric, and power-law ramps reach \(0.46\), \(0.43\), and \(0.40\) at CEM \(H=8\), respectively, while the slow-tempo ramp reaches only \(0.29\), below the next-step objective's \(0.31\).

The ordering of the curriculum also matters. The reverse ramp passes through the same profiles as the cutoff ramp and matches it at \(H=5\) (\(0.88\)), but because it ends on the next-step objective, it falls to \(0.16\) and \(0.04\) at \(H=8\) and \(10\), respectively, below the plain next-step model (\(0.31\) and \(0.15\)). At a fixed mean horizon, the geometric profile (\(0.86/0.42/0.25\) CEM) performs similarly to the uniform \(k=5\) objective (CEM \(0.85/0.45/0.27\), Figure 6). The far-only profile, which assigns no weight to horizons \(2,\dots,7\), fails at every horizon (\(\leq 0.10\) with both planners).

B · Qualitative rollout decoding

To visualize imagined rollouts, we train a separate decoder that maps the 384-d DINOv2 CLS embedding back to a 224×224 RGB image. The world model, including its (EMA) image encoder, stays frozen. The decoder has 13M parameters. We train on single frames from the training split for 40k steps with batch size 64, with AdamW. The decoder does not take proprioceptive inputs.

C · A toy model of the representation transition

Let \(o_t\in\mathcal O\) be observations, \(a_t\in\mathcal A\) actions, and \(\mathcal T:\mathcal O\times\mathcal A\to\mathcal O\) the environment dynamics. We learn an encoder \(E_\theta:\mathcal O\to\mathcal Z\) and a latent dynamics model \(T_\theta:\mathcal Z\times\mathcal A\to\mathcal Z\) that agree with the environment, \(E_\theta\big(\mathcal T(o_t,a_t)\big)=T_\theta\big(E_\theta(o_t),a_t\big)\), together with an anti-collapse term that keeps \(E_\theta\) non-trivial. Although \(\mathcal T\) acts one step at a time, we may equally learn an \(n\)-step map \(T^{(n)}_\theta(z_t,a_t,\dots,a_{t+n-1})=z_{t+n}\) that consumes a chunk of actions at once. The following example shows that this choice changes which features the encoder keeps, and in particular addresses the concern that JEPA learns slow features.

A two-feature model. Consider two independent AR(1) features \(x_t,y_t\) with their own action channels, a linear unit-variance encoder with a one-dimensional latent, and the optimal linear \(n\)-step predictor:

\[ \begin{aligned} x_{t+1} &= \lambda_x x_t + b_x a^x_t + \sigma_x \epsilon^x_t,\\ y_{t+1} &= \lambda_y y_t + b_y a^y_t + \sigma_y \epsilon^y_t,\\ z_t &= \cos\theta\, x_t+\sin\theta\, y_t,\\ \hat z_{t+n} &= p\, z_t + \sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\big(q^x_i a^x_{t+i} + q^y_i a^y_{t+i}\big), \end{aligned} \]

where \(a^{x,y}_t,\epsilon^{x,y}_t\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,1)\) and \(b^2+\sigma^2=1-\lambda^2\) so that \(x_t,y_t\) have unit variance. Each feature is characterized by its memory \(\lambda\) and its noise fraction \(\eta=\sigma^2/(b^2+\sigma^2)\): \(\eta=0\) is fully action-driven, \(\eta=1\) an uncontrollable distractor. Minimizing over \(p,\boldsymbol{q}\) leaves a loss in the encoder angle alone,

\[ L^{(n)}(\theta) = \ell_x(n)\cos^2\theta + \ell_y(n)\sin^2\theta + \Delta_n^2\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta, \quad \ell(n)=\eta\,(1-\lambda^{2n}),\; \Delta_n=\lambda_x^n-\lambda_y^n. \]

The first two terms are the irreducible error of each feature; the last is a mixing penalty. The encoder always commits to a single feature, \(\theta=0\) (\(x_t\)) or \(\theta=\pi/2\) (\(y_t\)), whichever has the smaller \(\ell(n)\). At \(n=1\), \(\ell(1)=\sigma^2\), so a slowly drifting feature (high \(\lambda\)) is nearly free whether it is driven by action or noise. As \(n\to\infty\), \(\ell\to\eta\): long-horizon prediction charges the fraction of a feature's motion that the actions do not explain. Moreover, the ratio \(\ell_x(n)/\ell_y(n)\) is monotone in \(n\) (increasing when \(\lambda_x>\lambda_y\)), so the preferred feature switches at most once: lengthening the horizon can only move the latent from the low-\(\sigma^2\) feature toward the low-\(\eta\) one.

The analogy with visual control is that agent and object motion is fast on the frame-skip timescale while background and lighting drift slowly (and uncontrollably). For a slow distractor \(x\) with memory time \(\tau_x=1/(1-\lambda_x)\gg 1\) and a fast feature \(y\) (\(\lambda_y^{2n}\approx 0\)), we have \(\ell_x(n)\approx 2\eta_x n/\tau_x\) for \(n\ll\tau_x\), so the switch occurs at \(n^\star\approx\eta_y\tau_x/(2\eta_x)\); beyond that, longer horizons change nothing. Averaging the loss uniformly over \(n\le k\), as we do in training, preserves this qualitative behavior: the averaged loss is still concave in \(\sin^2\theta\), so the encoder still commits to a single feature, and the switch moves to a larger \(k\).

D · Baselines

LeWM

LeWorldModel is an end-to-end trained joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA) that utilizes SIGReg to prevent latent collapse by encouraging the full embedding distribution to match an isotropic Gaussian. The LeWM training objective is defined as:

\[ \mathcal{L}_\text{LeWM} = \mathcal{L}_\text{pred} + \lambda\, \text{SIGReg}(\mathbf{Z}). \]

A complete table of hyperparameters can be found in Appendix E.3.

Temporal Straightening

Temporal Straightening keeps the DINOv2 encoder frozen and trains a small convolutional projector that maps its patch tokens to a compact \(14\times 14\times 8\) latent, in which the predictor is trained and planning takes place. A curvature regularizer encourages latent trajectories to be straight, so that Euclidean distance to the goal latent is a better planning objective:

\[ \mathcal{L}_\text{TS} = \mathcal{L}_\text{pred} + \lambda\, \mathcal{L}_\text{curv}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_\text{curv} = 1 - \cos\!\big(z_{t+1}-z_t,\; z_{t+2}-z_{t+1}\big), \]

averaged over consecutive latent triples. We train it as a next-step model with their \(\lambda=0.1\), keeping their relative weighting of the visual, proprioceptive and curvature terms, with the same predictor architecture, history length and data as our models, for two epochs. The original paper uses 8 latent channels, which we keep for Push-T, Cube and Reacher; on Maze we use a larger latent of 32 channels (\(14\times 14\times 32\)), since the 8-channel model plans poorly there. We evaluate it with our higher-budget open-loop protocol (Appendix E.2), not the planner budget of the original paper, which runs GD for 100 steps where ours runs 500 or more.

Fast LeWM

Fast LeWM builds on the LeWM encoder and objective, and replaces LeWM's recursive rollout with parallel prediction: a state-conditioned action encoder composes each prefix of the action sequence, and the predictor maps the current latent and the composed prefixes to all future latents in one pass. Like k-WM it avoids feeding predictions back into the predictor, but it does so with an additional action-composition module, whereas k-WM changes only the input layout and training target of the existing predictor. We re-run it on our datasets under the open-loop protocol of the main results, so the numbers are not comparable to the closed-loop MPC results reported in the original paper.

E · Hyperparameters

E.1 Predictor architecture

All predictors are causal transformers. The k-WM and next-step models of a class share the same architecture and differ only in the training sequence length.

E.2 Planning

The planner settings are shared by every method including the baselines. Both planners optimize actions in normalized units, and each episode is planned once and executed open-loop. CEM refits its sampling distribution to the top-\(k\) elites at each iteration. GD uses learning rate \(0.08\) for 500 steps on Push-T, Maze and Cube. On Reacher, 500 steps are not enough at any learning rate we tried, so every method uses learning rate \(0.01\) for 1999 steps.

E.3 LeWM

For LeWM, we select hyperparameters via a grid search over SIGReg (0.09, 0.16, 0.32, 0.48, 0.64, 0.8, 0.96) and training epochs (5, 20). We tune each method independently using the same search space and evaluation budget.

F · Environments and datasets

Push-T is a 2D continuous rigid-body manipulation task in which the agent (a blue dot) pushes a T-shaped block toward a specified goal configuration. We use the standard dataset from DINO-WM, containing 18,000 episodes that replay expert trajectories with varying levels of noise, with an average length of 196 steps.

Maze is a 2D navigation task in which an agent navigates through a maze. We adopt the D4RL environment with the Large Maze layout (9×12) and port it to MuJoCo 3. The training dataset consists of 500K procedurally generated frames. For rollout and planning, we use a BFS expert dataset consisting of trajectories toward randomly sampled reachable goals.

Reacher is a 2D robot-control task in which a two-joint robotic arm must reach a target joint configuration. We follow the DINO-WM environment setup and generate 1.9M frames with actions sampled uniformly from \([-5,5]\), with the environment running at 50 Hz. Rollouts use the same distribution as training, and we plan toward randomly sampled goals, since the small configuration space makes nearly all configurations mutually reachable.

OGBench Cube (Cube) is a 3D robot-manipulation task in which a robotic arm with a gripper picks up and places a cube, introduced in OGBench. We follow LeWM, using a dataset of 1.8M frames from scripted expert trajectories generated with their data-collection procedure. The rollout and planning dataset follows the same distribution as the training data.

Proprioception. Prior world-model work varies in how proprioceptive information is incorporated. To make comparisons across models more consistent, we provide the same proprioceptive information to our models and the corresponding baselines. For Push-T and Cube, we provide the world model with proprioceptive state in addition to visual observations: agent position and velocity for Push-T, and end-effector position and yaw for Cube. For Maze and Reacher, we provide velocity only. Providing absolute position would substantially reduce the role of visual state estimation in these environments, while omitting velocity would leave the state under-specified when rolling out from a single ground-truth frame.
